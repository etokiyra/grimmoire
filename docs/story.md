# The story of Grimmoire

This is the **narrative bible**: what the world is, what happened, who is in it,
what the player learns and in what order, and which questions are meant to stay
open. It is the reference the writing is checked against.

It is deliberately short on dates and long on consequences. Nothing here is
revealed to the player as a block of exposition; every fact below is earned by
examining something, by asking somebody, or by noticing that two accounts do not
match.

> **Status.** The prologue, the four characters who carry the spine (Ysolde,
> Ioreth, Vesper, Maren), Cassen's and Rin's threads, four new landmarks and eleven
> journal entries are **implemented**. Everything under *Unresolved* is **not**.
> See the "What is implemented" section at the end before trusting any of this.

## The heart of it

A village survived a drought by agreeing to feed the water. It worked for four
hundred years. Nobody alive remembers why it was ever allowed, and the institution
that was built to keep the agreement has spent that time making sure nobody
finishes the one sentence that would end it.

The cruelty is not that the village was evil. It is that the village was **kind**:
each generation agreed together, in daylight, and the person who went was asked,
and told, and mourned. The cruelty is what that arrangement did to the people who
kept it — and that when the water finally failed anyway, they did the thing one
more time out of habit, and it did not work, and the last person who said the
words over the offering has been standing in a ruin ever since, unable to admit
that she cannot tell the difference between keeping a promise and refusing to let
somebody go.

## What actually happened

**The first drought.** Generations back, the wells failed and the wood was dying.
The shrine's founder went down to the moonwell and asked it for water. It gave
water, and it asked for something in return, and the bargain that resulted was
made *after* the fact by a village that was desperate and agreed together. That
detail matters: it was not done to them.

**The office.** A covenant needs keeping. The shrine became an institution whose
whole purpose was to hold the ledger, choose when the water asked, say the words
over the person given, and — this is the part that turned it — make sure nobody
ever wrote down the word that ends the agreement. The catacomb wall is that
ledger. Hundreds of names, in one careful hand and then a worse one, because each
keeper inherited the list from the last.

**The scribe.** One of them, four lifetimes ago, started writing the moon's name
— the word you can ask it to stop with. She got two letters. The shrine's warden
at the time was ordered to stop her, and did, and buried her with her hands full
of wax and her stylus still sharp, so that everyone after would understand both
what the work was and what it cost. The lid is carved with two letters over and
over. The warden has been standing in the draught ever since, telling himself it
keeps the living warm.

**The failure.** Nine years ago the water went bad again, and then the well was
dry. The village — a generation that had forgotten the reason and remembered only
the rule — chose. The woman chosen wrote letters to the chapel every season and
sent none of them; the last says *I have told the moon.* She asked the water to
stop. She was given anyway. The water did not come back, and the village emptied
over the following year. The well's rope was cut at the top by a passing mage who
believed a dry well would stop the choosing. It hurried it instead.

**The aftermath.** The chapel's keeper said the words over her own sister. She is
still there. The basin she keeps is still full. She tells herself she is keeping
the chapel.

## The player

The wizard is a **wanderer with no history yet** — that is deliberate, and it is
the one thing about the protagonist left open for a later phase. What drives them
in this build is curiosity that keeps turning into responsibility: they arrive,
they look at things, and the things turn out to matter to people who are still
alive. The player never speaks, which is what lets them be the one who *asks* —
the whole story is assembled out of other people's answers.

## The cast

Each entry is: what they want, what they cannot say, and the contradiction that
makes them a person rather than a function.

| Who | Wants | Hides | Contradiction |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Ysolde** — the veiled one, who keeps the chapel | To be told it is over | That she said the words over her own sister, and that she has kept the rite alive out of habit | She calls her waiting *duty*. She is the one thing that would not let go. |
| **Ioreth** — priestess of the moonwell | To be left in peace with an office she did not ask for | That she holds the office she has concluded is wrong, and has never gone down to see what it was for | She is exact and careful and *unbelieving*. Her calm is exhaustion wearing a robe. |
| **Vesper** — what the catacombs kept | To be asked the right question | That it closed the scribe's lid while she was still writing, on orders, and has stood in the draught since by way of penance | Gentle, polite, and guilty of the worst act anybody in the story committed. |
| **Maren** — the wandering mage | To have said it out loud once | That she cut the well rope nine years ago to stop a choosing and hurried it instead | She let Cassen take the blame because the lie kept him talking to people, and she calls that mercy. |
| **Cassen** — of the hollow village | To be someone who matters | That he has known the truth about the rope since the second year, and that he kept a dead woman's letters for nine years | A liar who has been, quietly, the most reliable person left. |
| **Rin** — the lantern keeper | To keep the eleven lit and not think about the twelfth | Whose lantern the twelfth is (her mother's, who was given), and that she does not remember more than a week because she will not | Cheerful, forgetful, and keeping the wood's whole memory in eleven small fires. |
| **Wren** — of the quiet glade | For people to keep coming | That the glade was a floor before it was a wood: a shrine's threshold, and hers to keep | Leaves a light for Rin and lets her believe it is hers, because offering is easier than being thanked. |
| **Tamsin** — warden of the catacombs | To write the record properly | That she has counted the wall and knows what it is a tally of, and is afraid to say it in her own notes | Steel and gentleness. She is the only one still doing the job *well*, and the job is the problem. |
| **Nim** — apprentice of the moonwell | To be useful | That she has worked out what Ioreth is protecting and is frightened of it | Keeps charms and has never been asked what she is keeping out. |

## What the player learns, and in what order

1. **A place and a mood** — the ruined chapel, a full basin, a village that is
   "still there, the difference being who is doing the living in it".
2. **A dry well and a cut rope** — the village died of thirst, and somebody cut
   the rope from the *top*.
3. **Letters never sent** — a woman wrote to the chapel every season; the last
   line is *I have told the moon.*
4. **The dial** — the moon rises dark, fills, and lets go. The moonwell can be
   answered.
5. **The wall of names** — hundreds of them, in one hand and then a worse one.
   These are not burials. This is a *list*.
6. **The sealed door and Ioreth's office** — the shrine sealed something on
   purpose because it could not decide, and she inherited the decision.
7. **The scribe's lid** — two letters, over and over. Somebody was writing a word
   and was stopped.
8. **Ysolde's rite** — she said the words over her sister, and the water never came
   back. The player may then tell her it is over, which is the one thing she has
   been waiting nine years to be told.
9. **Vesper's confession** — it closed the lid while she was still writing. The
   player may tell it to come in out of the draught.

After that the past is legible and the remaining questions are not about what
happened but about what to do, which is where a later phase picks it up.

## Player choices and what they actually change

Five, all implemented, each a *story fact* saved by stable id:

| Choice | Saved as | Visible consequence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Tell Ysolde the water never came back | `ysolde-released` | Her greeting changes for good; she acknowledges the difference between keeping a thing and keeping it alive. |
| Tell her the moon's answer was yours | `ysolde-told` | Recorded; she treats the moonwell as somebody's doing rather than the weather. |
| Tell Vesper to come in from the draught | `vesper-told` | Its greeting changes; it stops standing in the doorway. |
| Tell Cassen you know he cut the rope | `cassen-accused` | He stops lying about it, and becomes noticeably more useful about everything else. |
| Say nothing, and let him wonder | `cassen-befriended` | A warmer exchange, and he admits why he kept the letters. |
| Tell Maren the truth | `maren-confessed` | Cassen's dialogue changes: he reveals he has known for eight years. |
| Tell him to give the letters back | `letters-promised` | Ysolde's `returned` node: she reads one and refuses the rest. |

There is no morality meter and no relationship score. A flag is a fact the world
remembers, not a number it accumulates.

## Intentionally unresolved

- **The moon's name.** Two letters exist; the word does not. Do not write it down.
  The moment it is known, the bargain has an ending, and that is a *later* story.
- **What the moonwell is.** It drinks, it answers, it gave a door back. Whether it
  is alive, indifferent, or an arrangement that outlived its meaning is left open
  on purpose. Ioreth's "very little of this is mine to explain" is the game's own
  position.
- **Where the wizard came from.** No history, no reason for arriving. The prologue
  shows the player watching the story, not inside it.
- **Whether the water could have come back.** Ysolde's sister asked the moon to
  stop, and the water did not return. Whether that is refusal, or whether the
  bargain had simply already lapsed, is never answered.
- **What is behind the sealed door.** It opens as a shortcut and nothing more.

## Rules for writing in this world

- **People first.** Nobody explains the plot. Every revelation is somebody's
  answer to a question the player chose to ask.
- **Short lines.** Two to four at a time, never a speech. The graph enforces it.
- **Contradicting accounts are a feature.** Cassen's lie and Maren's confession are
  both true in their own way; the player can hold both.
- **No cartoon evil.** The shrine is an institution doing what it was built to do,
  and it is wrong, and everyone in it is tired.
- **Silence counts.** "Say nothing" is an option in almost every exchange, and it
  is never punished.
- **The cruelty must cost the cruel.** Ysolde's keeping is love that will not let
  go; Ioreth's office is a life spent not looking; Vesper's penance is standing
  still. Nobody enjoys any of it.

## What is implemented, and what is not

**Implemented:** the five-scene prologue (`ui::opening`); the eleven journal
entries; four new landmarks (the worn sigil, the wall of names, the sealed door,
the twelfth lantern); Ysolde's, Ioreth's, Vesper's, Maren's, Cassen's and Rin's
new material; the two new flags and the two newly-read ones; and the tests that
keep every conversation a sound graph.

**Not implemented, and not promised:** no second chapter; no ending; no resolution
of the moon's name; Tamsin, Nim and Wren have the material the previous phase gave
them and one thread each that stops short of the spine; the wizard's own history;
and any consequence beyond a changed line of dialogue — nothing in this build
changes a location, opens a place, or alters the puzzle.
